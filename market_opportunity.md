# Market Opportunity: AI-Assisted Clinical-Trial Prescreening

**Primary client:** Mid-sized multi-site clinical research site network

**Consulting perspective:** AI consultant and integration specialist

**Document status:** Initial external research and client-discovery framework

**Date:** 2 September 2026

## 1. Executive conclusion

A multi-site clinical research network is a credible primary client for an AI-assisted prescreening solution because it owns or operates the coordinator workflow, has legitimate access to candidate information under an appropriate governance model, and can measure the operational effect across multiple sites.

The opportunity is not to automate eligibility or enrolment decisions. It is to reduce repetitive criterion-level review, make uncertainty visible, preserve evidence and rationale, and route difficult cases to qualified staff. The commercial case should initially be based on coordinator capacity and screening throughput, not on broad claims about the size of the AI market.

External evidence establishes that:

- clinical-trial activity is large and sustained: ClinicalTrials.gov reported 593,857 registered studies and 65,004 recruiting studies as of 14 July 2026;[1]
- Europe has an active and increasingly transparent trial environment: CTIS had received more than 13,000 initial applications since 2022 and more than 10,600 trials had been authorised by EU Member States by 2025;[2]
- research sites face staff-capacity and technology-fragmentation pressures; a global site survey reported that 63% of responding sites had difficulty hiring and retaining employees, while 84% believed technology could improve efficiency and recruitment or retention;[3]
- AI-assisted matching can reduce review effort in research settings, but the evidence is heterogeneous and not yet strong enough to assume production benefits without a controlled pilot.[4][5]

The correct next commercial step is therefore a paid discovery and controlled pilot, followed by an ROI decision using measured client data.

## 2. How this market research was conducted

### 2.1 Start with the client decision

The research question is not simply “Is clinical-trial AI a growing market?” The useful question is:

> Should a mid-sized multi-site research network invest in a controlled pilot of AI-assisted prescreening, and what evidence would justify wider deployment?

This framing keeps the research connected to a purchase decision.

### 2.2 Use an evidence hierarchy

| Evidence level | Best use | Examples | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Regulators and public registries | Market activity, legal context, public trial counts | EMA, CTIS, ClinicalTrials.gov | Does not prove client ROI |
| Peer-reviewed research | Technical feasibility, measured workflow effects, known limitations | TrialGPT, systematic reviews | Often retrospective, synthetic, or disease-specific |
| Professional associations | Site workload, staffing and operating conditions | SCRS surveys | Survey samples may not represent every site network |
| Conference abstracts | Recent operational benchmarks | ASCO site-network studies | Usually limited methods and no full peer review |
| Vendor reports and case studies | Competitor capabilities and evidence of adoption | Tempus, Deep 6 AI, TrialX | Commercially interested source; results may not generalise |
| Client operational data | Final ROI, workload and deployment decision | CTMS logs, time study, screening logs, payroll | Must be collected during discovery |

No external benchmark should be presented as if it were the client’s own performance.

### 2.3 Triangulate important claims

A strong claim should ideally have:

1. an independent source showing that the problem exists;
2. an operational example showing that a solution can work;
3. client data showing the size of the local opportunity.

For example, published research may show that AI can reduce screening effort, while a client time study establishes how many hours are actually available to save.

### 2.4 Record assumptions separately from facts

Every number used later in the ROI model should be classified as one of:

- **Observed:** measured directly from the client;
- **Contracted:** taken from a quote, salary record, licence agreement or cloud price;
- **External benchmark:** supported by a cited third-party source;
- **Assumption:** a provisional value requiring validation.

This prevents an illustrative calculation from becoming an unsupported promise.

## 3. Market and operating context

### 3.1 Trial activity creates a recurring screening workload

ClinicalTrials.gov reported 593,857 registered studies, including 65,004 recruiting studies, as of 14 July 2026.[1] These are global ecosystem figures rather than an addressable-market estimate, but they demonstrate a sustained volume of active research.

In Europe, the Clinical Trials Information System is the common regulatory entry point for clinical trials. EMA reported that CTIS had received more than 13,000 initial applications since its 2022 launch, including 2,844 during 2025, and that Member States had authorised more than 10,600 trials.[2] These figures support the strategic relevance of trial-operational technology in the European market; they do not indicate how many organisations will buy this particular solution.

### 3.2 Site networks have a measurable capacity problem

Research-site teams combine recruitment, patient coordination, trial documentation, sponsor communication and system administration. In a 2022 global survey of more than 500 sites, 63% reported difficulty hiring and retaining employees, 43% reported lower-than-average enrolment, and 84% believed technology could positively affect efficiency and recruitment or retention.[3] The survey was sponsored by a technology provider and was predominantly US-based, so it is directional evidence rather than a European benchmark.

The practical business problem is constrained coordinator time. Prescreening requires staff to repeatedly compare patient information with lengthy inclusion and exclusion criteria, document findings, identify missing information and escalate uncertain cases. Across a network, variation between sites can also reduce consistency and management visibility.

### 3.3 AI evidence is promising but immature

The NIH TrialGPT research reported 87.3% matching accuracy and a 42.6% reduction in screening time in its evaluated research setting.[5] The underlying work used synthetic patient profiles and controlled evaluation, so these figures should not be used as a promised client benefit.

A 2024 scoping review examined 51 studies of AI in clinical-trial recruitment and retention. It found reported benefits in efficiency, cost, recruitment and accuracy, but also highlighted privacy, security, transparency, bias, limited generalisability and insufficient external validation. The authors concluded that the field remains at an early stage of maturity and needs stronger, standardised evidence.[4]

This supports a human-in-the-loop pilot with explicit safety gates. It does not support autonomous eligibility decisions.

## 4. Why the primary client is a multi-site research network

| Client characteristic | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Direct control of site workflows | The network can change how coordinators prescreen and review candidates. |
| Lawful access to source information | Unlike a detached technology vendor, the network may already process candidate information for trial operations, subject to purpose, legal-basis and access-control review. |
| Repeated work across sites | A common workflow can create reusable value and more consistent documentation. |
| Multiple active studies | A broader study portfolio creates recurring patient-to-criterion comparisons. |
| Central operations or quality team | Central ownership can manage configuration, monitoring, validation and incident response. |
| Measurable outcomes | CTMS and screening logs can support before/after measurement across sites. |

The initial buyer is likely to be a head of clinical operations, site-network COO, recruitment lead or innovation/digital-transformation lead. Clinical investigators, coordinators, data protection, quality, IT security and legal teams are essential stakeholders, even when they are not the budget owner.

## 5. Proposed value proposition

### 5.1 Narrow value proposition

> Help coordinators prepare criterion-level prescreening reviews faster and more consistently, while preserving cited evidence, surfacing uncertainty and requiring human confirmation.

### 5.2 Problems addressed

- repetitive reading and comparison work;
- inconsistent documentation between staff or sites;
- difficulty locating the source sentences supporting a conclusion;
- missed or delayed escalation of unknown information;
- limited visibility into review volume, uncertainty and workload;
- manual handoff to a human-review queue.

### 5.3 Explicit exclusions

The solution does not:

- determine final eligibility;
- enrol or reject participants;
- replace investigator or coordinator judgement;
- infer undocumented clinical facts;
- contact patients automatically;
- justify production use using synthetic-data accuracy alone.

These limits are part of the commercial proposition because they reduce implementation and safety risk.

## 6. Competitive and alternative landscape

The client has more alternatives than simply “buy this AI tool” or “do nothing.”

| Alternative | Strength | Limitation or opportunity for this proposal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manual prescreening | Familiar and flexible | Labour-intensive; variable documentation; limited scaling |
| CTMS/EHR filters and reports | Uses existing systems and structured fields | Often weak on narrative notes, temporal criteria and explanations |
| Rules-based matching | Deterministic and auditable | Expensive to maintain; brittle across protocols and wording |
| Enterprise matching platform | Broad integration, support and mature product functions | Potentially high cost, long procurement and vendor lock-in |
| Custom human-in-the-loop assistant | Narrow workflow fit, transparent outputs and staged integration | Requires local validation, governance, maintenance and clear ownership |

Examples of established or adjacent offerings include Tempus/Deep 6 AI, WCG-linked recruitment services and TrialX. Company-reported materials show that commercial platforms combine patient matching with network, referral, activation or analytics capabilities.[6][7][8] These sources establish market activity and buyer expectations, but their performance claims must be treated as vendor claims.

A 2024 ASCO abstract describing 16 Exigent Research Network sites reported 33,083 algorithm-generated matches reviewed by nurses over two years, requiring 6,617 nursing hours. This equals approximately 12 minutes of nurse review per match and demonstrates that automation can create a large human-review workload rather than eliminate it.[9] It is a useful external benchmark, not a default assumption for this client.

## 7. Bottom-up opportunity model

The business opportunity should be estimated from the client’s workflow, not from a commercial market-size report.

### 7.1 Core workload equations

```text
Monthly candidate-trial reviews
= candidate records reviewed per month × average trials considered per candidate

Current monthly prescreening hours
= monthly candidate-trial reviews × current minutes per review ÷ 60

Monthly hours released
= current monthly prescreening hours × measured time-reduction rate

Annual capacity value
= monthly hours released × 12 × loaded hourly staff cost

Incremental contribution value
= additional completed enrolments × contribution margin per enrolment

Annual quantified benefit
= capacity value + incremental contribution value + validated avoided costs
```

“Hours released” should not automatically be described as cash savings. It becomes a cash saving only if overtime, contractors or headcount are actually reduced. Otherwise, it is capacity that may be redeployed to recruitment, participant support, quality work or additional studies.

### 7.2 Data required from the client

| Variable | Preferred source | Collection method | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Number of sites and active recruiting trials | CTMS / portfolio report | Export trailing 12 months | Defines deployment and workload scale |
| Candidate records reviewed | Screening logs / referral system | Monthly count by site and study | Primary activity volume |
| Candidate-trial pairs assessed | Screening logs | Count pairwise reviews, not unique patients only | Closest unit to AI-assisted work |
| Coordinator minutes per pair | Direct time study | Sample 30–50 reviews across sites and trial types | Establishes current labour burden |
| Coordinator loaded hourly cost | Finance / HR | Salary + employer costs + applicable overhead | Converts time to economic value |
| Referral-to-prescreen conversion | CTMS / recruitment funnel | Cohort by study | Shows funnel quality |
| Prescreen-to-consent and consent-to-enrol rates | CTMS | Cohort by study and site | Tests downstream value |
| Screen failures and reasons | CTMS / source review | Structured reason analysis | Identifies avoidable waste and data gaps |
| Recruitment delays | Study plans and actuals | Compare target and actual milestones | Supports schedule value, if causally defensible |
| Revenue or contribution per enrolment | Contracts / finance | Use contribution margin, not sponsor payment alone | Supports incremental-value calculation |
| Current software and integration costs | Procurement / IT | Contracts and support effort | Avoids understating total cost |
| Error, rework and escalation rates | Quality logs | Sample audit | Establishes risk and quality baseline |

### 7.3 How to obtain real numbers

Use three complementary methods:

1. **System extracts:** use 6–12 months of CTMS, referral and screening data to measure volume and conversion.
2. **Time-and-motion sample:** coordinators record start/end time and outcome for a representative sample. Include straightforward, uncertain and complex cases across sites.
3. **Structured interviews:** ask coordinators where time is spent and where data are missing, then verify interview claims against logs.

Run a short baseline measurement before introducing the assistant. During the pilot, capture the same measures plus model label, human decision, correction reason, handling time and escalation outcome.

## 8. Initial measurement and pilot design

### 8.1 Recommended sequence

| Stage | Purpose | Evidence produced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery | Confirm workflow, data access, buyer, risks and baseline | Process map, data inventory, baseline metrics |
| Retrospective validation | Test on approved historical cases | Accuracy, review-routing, subgroup and error analysis |
| Silent pilot | Run without influencing decisions | Real workflow fit and prospective safety evidence |
| Assisted pilot | Allow coordinators to use outputs with mandatory confirmation | Time, correction, adoption and workload measures |
| Scale decision | Compare realised benefit with total cost and risk | Go, revise or stop decision |

### 8.2 Pilot KPIs

The pilot should report at least:

- median coordinator time per candidate-trial review;
- percentage reduction in preparation time;
- human agreement by label and criterion type;
- unsafe false-positive count;
- review-routing recall for uncertain or non-applicable cases;
- review-queue volume and median handling time;
- percentage of outputs corrected by reviewers;
- cases not assessable because required data were missing;
- coordinator adoption and override reasons;
- referral, consent and enrolment funnel metrics, reported separately from model accuracy;
- total operating cost per reviewed pair and per coordinator-approved candidate.

The current synthetic proof of concept demonstrates the workflow but not production performance. On the locked 120-assessment evaluation, GPT-4.1 achieved 72.5% exact agreement and GPT-5.6 Sol achieved 65.0%; the more expensive model did not improve the result. This is evidence that model price alone is not the solution and that prompt, label semantics, data quality and local validation matter.

## 9. Commercial opportunity and buyer case

### 9.1 Value categories

| Value category | Credible measurement | Avoided overclaim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coordinator capacity | Measured hours released | Do not call all released time cash savings |
| Faster review turnaround | Median time from referral to completed prescreen | Do not claim faster enrolment without funnel evidence |
| More complete coverage | Additional trials or candidates reviewed with same staff | Do not equate review with enrolment |
| Consistent documentation | Audit completeness and reduced rework | Do not equate explanation with correctness |
| Earlier uncertainty escalation | Review-routing recall and queue time | Do not imply uncertain cases are clinically resolved |
| Incremental enrolment | Controlled before/after or matched-study analysis | Avoid attributing every new enrolment to AI |

### 9.2 Recommended commercial offer

The initial offer should be a bounded discovery and pilot package rather than a network-wide licence promise. It should include:

- current-state process and data assessment;
- baseline measurement;
- privacy, security and regulatory classification;
- integration design;
- retrospective and silent validation;
- coordinator-facing pilot with human confirmation;
- benefit, cost and risk report;
- scale recommendation and production roadmap.

Pricing and duration should be separated into:

1. discovery;
2. technical pilot;
3. controlled operational pilot;
4. optional production integration and support.

The existing eight-week, approximately EUR 23,000 estimate may remain a technical proof-of-concept estimate, but it should not be presented as the total price of an integrated multi-site pilot. Real deployment also requires client discovery, governance, security, integration, validation, training, change management and post-launch support.

## 10. Key risks affecting the opportunity

- incomplete or outdated patient data can create incorrect assessments;
- ambiguous criteria and inconsistent reference labels can distort measured accuracy;
- a system that abstains frequently may reduce safety risk but create excessive review workload;
- site-to-site workflow and data variation may limit scalability;
- EHR/CTMS integration, identity and access management may cost more than model inference;
- sensitive health-data processing requires a lawful basis, data minimisation, security controls and a DPIA assessment;
- automation bias may cause staff to accept plausible but incorrect rationales;
- benefits may fail to materialise if the assistant adds another disconnected interface;
- vendor and model changes require monitoring and revalidation;
- participant access and fairness must be assessed across relevant demographic and clinical groups.

These risks will be developed formally in `roi_risk_assessment.md`, `eu_ai_act_compliance.md` and `gdpr_documentation.md`.

## 11. Decision gates

Proceed from discovery to pilot only if:

- the client identifies a named business owner and clinical owner;
- a lawful, minimised data flow is feasible;
- baseline workload is large enough to justify intervention;
- representative historical cases and reviewer capacity are available;
- integration effort is proportionate to expected value;
- success and stop criteria are agreed before testing.

Proceed from pilot to scale only if:

- no agreed safety stop criterion is breached;
- time or throughput improvement is measured in the real workflow;
- reviewer workload remains manageable;
- model performance is acceptable across relevant sites and subgroups;
- total benefit exceeds the full operating and governance cost under conservative assumptions;
- accountable clinical, operational, privacy, security and quality owners approve deployment.

## 12. Information still required from the client

The following questions should be answered during discovery:

1. How many sites and active recruiting studies are in scope?
2. Which therapeutic areas and trial phases dominate?
3. How are candidates identified today: EHR search, referrals, registries, advertising or manual lists?
4. How many candidate-trial reviews are completed each month at each site?
5. Which roles perform each screening step, and at what loaded cost?
6. How long do simple, uncertain and complex reviews take?
7. What are current referral, prescreen, screen, consent and enrolment conversion rates?
8. Where are the source data stored, and which systems would require integration?
9. Which countries and legal entities control or process the data?
10. What are the current bottlenecks, quality incidents and audit requirements?
11. What financial value does the network assign to released capacity and incremental enrolment?
12. What minimum benefit and maximum risk would justify wider deployment?

Until these data are collected, the opportunity is substantiated but the ROI is not.

## 13. Evidence quality and limitations

- Global and EU trial counts show ecosystem scale, not the number of prospective buyers.
- Published performance studies use different tasks, labels, datasets and outcome measures; their accuracy figures are not directly comparable.
- Much clinical-trial matching research is retrospective, synthetic, oncology-focused or US-based.
- Vendor case studies demonstrate adoption and possible operating models but are not independent proof of benefit.
- Recruitment results depend on protocol design, patient population, site capacity, awareness, referrals and participant preferences—not only matching technology.
- The client’s own baseline and controlled pilot are the authoritative sources for the investment decision.
